A personal site is easy to misunderstand. It can look like a museum: a clean room containing only the work that survived. I would rather mine behaved like a workbench.

The distinction matters. A museum waits for completion. A workbench makes room for the part that is still awkward: the project note before the project has a slogan, the small tool before anyone calls it a company, the daily entry that records what changed and what did not.

That is the kind of record I want to keep here. Not a feed optimized for urgency, and not a portfolio optimized for pretending every experiment had a master plan.

## Make the trail useful

Publishing is not only about announcing a result. It is a way to leave the next version of yourself a map.

When a project is public, I can point to the constraint that shaped it: why [Sable](/blog/sable) cares about binary size, why [Kinetic](/blog/kinetic) starts in simulation, or why [wolfpdf](/blog/wolfpdf) takes a document format seriously enough to put a game inside it. The artifact is more useful when the reasoning around it is available too.

That trail also gives other people something concrete to react to. “I am interested in robotics” is hard to answer. A simulator, a write-up, or a failed prototype gives a conversation an edge. Someone can disagree with the approach, show a better tool, or recognize a problem they have already met.

The goal is not to publish every thought. It is to publish enough context that finished work does not arrive as a magic trick.

## A site should lower the cost of return

The useful unit of public work is rarely a grand launch. More often, it is a return:

1. Build a small, inspectable version.
2. Write down the decision that mattered.
3. Link the work where someone can find it again.
4. Come back when the next experiment changes the answer.

This is why the site has essays, shorter blog posts, and daily notes. They are different resolutions of the same practice. An essay earns its length when the idea needs room. A project note captures the engineering while it is still fresh. A daily entry preserves a small fact before it dissolves into “I think that happened last month.”

The structure is deliberately plain. Markdown files are easier to keep than a publishing workflow that requires ceremony. Stable links are better than a timeline that disappears under the next update. Machine-readable pages matter because a reader is not always a person in a browser.

## Public does not mean performative

Building in public can become a pressure to narrate progress before there is anything to say. I do not find that very useful. There is already too much grift around public work. The point is not constant visibility; it is durable evidence.

I want to share the details that make a project legible: the constraint, the tradeoff, the result, and the next question. Sometimes that is a clean release. Sometimes it is a note that says the model was wrong, the hardware was late, or the idea became less interesting after a week of contact with reality.

That is still progress. Honest artifacts compound better than polished claims.

## Leave room for the next build

I build from Khagaria, often across robotics, systems software, and research. Those fields can feel far apart until you look at the loop underneath: make a claim, build the smallest thing that can test it, observe the failure, and improve the claim.

This site is part of that loop. It is where the work gets a name, a link, and enough context to travel beyond my laptop. If something here helps another person start, inspect, or challenge an idea, the workbench is doing its job.
